# Cinematic dubbing: listening demos and the evaluation plan

Status: **demos built, nobody has listened to them yet.** Everything below that is a number comes
from offline signal measurements. They describe what the audio contains. They do not say whether it
is better. That needs people (protocol in section 6).

## 1. The audio

`runtime/bench/dubbing/listening/index.html` is a player (open it in a browser, use headphones).
It has four scenarios times five presentations, 20 MP3s. They are regenerated by

```
cd runtime && python scripts/dubbing_demo.py --out bench/dubbing/listening [--blind]
```

No network, no providers, no credits. `--blind` also writes `blind/` (shuffled names, `key.json`;
git-ignored) for a listening session.

**Source material.** The Korean speech and the English translation are clips from `bench/samples`
(the benchmark corpus). I picked five sentence pairs that mean the same thing in both languages
(for example "저기요, 공항까지 가는 가장 빠른 길이 어디인가요?" and "Excuse me, What is the fastest way
to get to the airport from here?"). Every presentation of a scenario uses the identical speech and the
identical translation signal (a test checks this). Clips are level-matched, so a difference you hear
is the presentation, not the recording level.

**Caveat that matters.** Both voices are synthetic. The "original" in these demos does not carry the
emotion of a real person, so the demos can show timing, level and transitions but cannot show whether
real human presence builds trust. For the listening study, record real speakers.

| scenario | what happens |
|---|---|
| `short` | One short utterance ("There will be 6 of us, do you have a table?"). |
| `sentence` | One sentence. The original ends before the translation begins. |
| `continuous` | Three sentences with 0.5 s pauses. The speaker talks on while the first is being translated. |
| `interrupted` | The translation of the first sentence is cut off one second in; the speaker goes on. |

Translation timing follows the measured pipeline: the translation starts 1.26 s after the speaker
stops, and a second translation waits for the first to finish. The translation is never shortened or
moved to line up with the original.

## 2. The five presentations and how each changes the experience

| presentation | what the listener hears |
|---|---|
| `ai_only` | Silence while the person speaks and for about 1.3 s after, then the translation. Today's product. |
| `live_balanced` | The shipped prototype. The real voice is heard as the person speaks (about 0.3 to 0.5 s late, at about -10 dB). When the translation starts it ducks to about -20 dB over 120 ms, and comes back over 500 ms after. |
| `replay_brief` | Exploratory. As `ai_only`, but when the translation starts, the first 2 s of the original are replayed under it at -12 dB, sinking to -22 dB within 150 ms and fading out. |
| `replay_full` | Exploratory. The whole original is replayed under the translation (-22 dB), faded out at the end, never longer than the translation. |
| `live_plus_brief` | Exploratory. `live_subtle` (the quieter profile) plus the brief replay. The listener hears the source language twice. |

The two replay variants are **not** production code. They need an utterance paired with its
translation (the live path only mixes streams) and a buffer of the whole utterance. Do not build
them unless listening says they are worth it.

## 3. What the numbers say

Measured on the rendered mixes, per scenario (time in seconds). "Silence" is the time in which
nothing is audible (windows below -50 dBFS) from the start until the last translation ends.

| scenario / presentation | first sound after start | silence | longest silence | original audible | original alone | original with translation | translation over original, median / worst 10% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| sentence / ai_only | 6.2 | 7.4 | 6.2 | 0 | 0 | 0 | - |
| sentence / live_balanced | 0.8 | 4.3 | 1.9 | 3.1 | 3.1 | 0 | no overlap |
| sentence / replay_brief | 6.2 | 7.4 | 6.2 | 1.0 | 0.1 | 0.9 | 22.9 / 16.1 dB |
| sentence / replay_full | 6.2 | 7.2 | 6.2 | 2.6 | 0.4 | 2.2 | 21.9 / 14.3 dB |
| continuous / ai_only | 6.5 | 12.8 | 6.5 | 0 | 0 | 0 | - |
| continuous / live_balanced | 0.8 | 8.2 | 2.0 | 10.3 | 5.3 | 5.0 | 17.9 / 11.6 dB |
| continuous / replay_brief | 6.5 | 12.7 | 6.5 | 3.7 | 0.4 | 3.3 | 22.2 / 18.3 dB |
| continuous / replay_full | 6.5 | 12.0 | 6.5 | 9.4 | 1.7 | 7.7 | 20.7 / 13.6 dB |
| continuous / live_plus_brief | 0.7 | 8.6 | 2.0 | 10.9 | 5.2 | 5.7 | 21.0 / 14.9 dB |

(All scenarios and all columns are in `bench/dubbing/listening/metrics.json`. "First sound" counts
from t = 0; the speaker starts at 0.3 s.)

What this shows, and what it does not:

1. **For an isolated sentence the live original does not overlap the translation at all, yet it is the
   biggest change in the experience.** It fills the time the person is speaking. With `ai_only` the
   listener hears nothing for 6.2 s (4.5 s of speech plus the pipeline wait); with the live original
   the longest silence is 1.9 s. The overlap the goal worries about is missing, but the dead air, which
   may matter more for feeling that someone is on the line, is mostly gone. The replay variants do
   nothing about the dead air. They only add the original under the translation.
2. **Roughly 1.3 s of silence between the end of the original and the start of the translation remains
   in every presentation.** That gap is the pipeline latency. It is real and unchanged by design. Whether
   it feels natural (a translator pausing to render) or odd is a listening question.
3. **Overlap only happens when the speaker talks on.** In `continuous` the original and translation are
   audible together for 5.0 s (live) to 7.7 s (full replay) of a 13 s clip. There the live prototype keeps the
   translation a median 17.9 dB above the original, 11.6 dB in the worst 10% of 100 ms windows (the
   moment the duck is still closing). Replays are cleaner, 13.6 to 18.3 dB at the low end, because their
   level is fixed and known in advance.
4. **Share of played energy that is the original voice: 5% to 14% (live, depending on scenario), 0.2% to
   0.8% (replays).** This is also a rough echo proxy: whatever the listener's speaker plays can leak
   into the listener's own microphone. The live original plays alone (no translation) for 2 to 6 s per
   clip, which is when it would be the only thing leaking, in the other language.
5. **No clipping in any presentation** (peaks 0.26 to 0.35 of full scale).

## 4. Telephone-style codecs (the weakest point of the cinematic idea)

On a real call the mix is encoded before it reaches the listener. I passed every mix through 8 kHz
G.711 mu-law and through Opus voice mode at 24 and 12 kbit/s and compared the original voice with the
codec's own error. The table gives the share of the time the original is audible in which it stands
at least 6 dB above that error (higher is better). It is a crude proxy for "is it still there", not a
perceptual model.

| presentation | mu-law | Opus 24 kbit/s | Opus 12 kbit/s |
|---|---|---|---|
| live_balanced | 100% | 42% to 86% | 39% to 84% |
| live_plus_brief | 98% to 100% | 40% to 62% | 33% to 59% |
| replay_brief | 90% to 100% | 6% to 11% | 0% to 10% |
| replay_full | 100% | 5% to 14% | 5% to 11% |

Interpretation: a voice under the translation at -20 dB or lower is below what a voice codec keeps at
low bitrates. In the low-bitrate cases the quiet layer is mostly noise-shaped away or turned into
artifacts. Replays, which sit entirely under the translation, suffer most; the live prototype survives
better because it plays alone at -10 dB between translations. This favours designs where the original is
loud enough, or heard alone, at least briefly. The LiveKit bitrate used in production is not measured
here: check the real encoder settings before relying on these numbers.

## 5. Answers to the questions in the goal (what offline work can and cannot answer)

- **Do isolated sentences matter?** Structurally, the overlap is absent. The measurable effect of the
  live original is the removal of most of the 5 to 7 s of dead air before the first translation, not overlap. Whether that is what
  listeners value is untested.
- **Does the original make the call more human?** Untested, and these demos cannot test it (synthetic voices).
- **Do simultaneous languages interfere with comprehension?** Untested. The worst-10% translation-to-original
  ratios (about 11 to 18 dB) are in a range where speech in another language is usually still easy to
  ignore, but that is an assumption from audio engineering, not a result. A listener who understands both
  languages will be distracted much more than one who understands only the translation.
- **Heard continuously or briefly?** The replays are the brief/continuous test: `replay_brief` against `replay_full`.
  The live prototype is "continuous while the person speaks", so it is the third point.
- **Interruptions and overlap:** the cut-off translation takes its replay with it (short fade), and the
  live original keeps its normal behaviour. No artifact or clipping in the rendered clips. How it feels is untested.
- **Could users mistake the translated voice for an AI caller rather than a person using a translation?**
  Untested. This is the core product question and needs the listener to answer it in their own words
  (protocol, measure T3).

## 6. Recommended first listening test

**Conditions (3):** `ai_only`, `live_balanced`, `replay_brief`. Reasons: `ai_only` is the baseline;
`live_balanced` is the only presentation that exists in the product; `replay_brief` tests the documentary
idea with the least overlap. `replay_full` and `live_plus_brief` stay out of the first round: they overlap
more or play the source language twice, and the first round should isolate the one question that
changes the architecture.

**Material.** Real recorded speakers (several voices, both genders, some emotion), the same sentence pairs,
translation by the actual pipeline recorded once and reused (no live variation), so every condition has the
identical translation. Ten to twelve clips per condition across `short`, `sentence`, `continuous`, `interrupted`.

**Listeners.** At least 24 people who understand the translation language (English) and **not** the speaker's
language; 8 or more of a second group who understand both (the case where the original is most distracting).
Headphones for everyone; a second round on a phone speaker.

**Design.** Within subjects. Each listener hears every condition for every scenario, in a counterbalanced
(Latin-square) order, clip order randomised, condition names hidden (use the `--blind` output).

**Measures, per clip unless stated:**

| id | what | how |
|---|---|---|
| I1 | Intelligibility | 2 to 3 comprehension questions about the translation (correct/incorrect), plus a transcription of a number or name |
| I2 | Perceived clarity | "How easy was it to understand the translation?" 1 to 7 |
| N1 | Naturalness of transitions | "How natural was the change from the person to the translation?" 1 to 7 |
| N2 | Distraction | "Did the other voice distract you?" 1 to 7 |
| H1 | Human presence | "It felt like a real person was talking to me" 1 to 7 |
| T1 | Trust | "I would trust this to get an important message across" 1 to 7 |
| T3 | Caller identity | Open question first: "Who or what is talking to you?" then "A person using a translation tool / an AI assistant / unsure" |
| E1 | Effort | "How much effort did it take?" 1 to 7 |
| P1 | Preference (end of block) | Rank the three versions of the same scenario; free comment |
| O1 | Behaviour (optional) | Time to respond / repeat requests in an interactive version |

**Decision rule (set before the test).**
- Intelligibility must not be worse than `ai_only` by more than 3 percentage points on I1 and 0.5 points on I2
  (non-inferiority, paired). If it is worse, dubbing is out whatever else it does.
- Dubbing is worth continuing if it improves H1 and T1 by at least 0.5 point with a preference for it from at
  least 60% of listeners, **and** N2 and E1 do not rise by more than 0.5.
- Analyse with Wilcoxon signed-rank (or a paired t-test for large samples) and report effect sizes with
  confidence intervals; use Friedman across the three conditions for the preference ranking. Report the
  both-languages group separately.
- Include a catch clip (a deliberately broken mix) to remove inattentive listeners.

**Next round, based on the first:** only if `replay_brief` beats `live_balanced` would it be worth
designing the pairing and buffering it needs (section 2). Only if `live_balanced` beats `ai_only` is it
worth tuning its levels (the duck depth is the main trade-off; see `docs/dubbing.md`).

## 7. Technical limits that affect real phone calls

1. **Codec.** Section 4. Low-bitrate voice codecs remove or distort a voice at -20 dB under another.
   A caller on a phone line or low-bandwidth link may not hear the original layer at all; the system should
   not rely on it for anything.
2. **Handset and app processing.** Noise suppression, automatic gain control and echo cancellation on the
   listener's device treat a quiet second voice as noise. Not measured.
3. **Echo into the listener's speech-to-text.** On speaker-phone, the played mix leaks into the listener's
   microphone. Today only the translation leaks; with dubbing the speaker's original voice leaks as well,
   in the other language, and plays alone for 2 to 6 s per clip at -10 dB. Test with a real speakerphone before
   exposing it. The feature is meant for headphones.
4. **Time offset.** The live original arrives about 0.3 s after the speaker's lips move; on a video call that is visible.
   A replay lines up with the translation, not with the lips.
5. **Real voices vary.** Levels differ between speakers and microphones; the demos normalise them, the product does not.
6. **Synthetic stand-ins.** The demos use synthetic voices; real emotion, breathing, background noise and
   crosstalk are absent.
7. **Gap before the translation.** About 1.3 s with no sound stays in every presentation. The original does not hide it.

## 8. Recommendation

- **Default stays AI-only** until listening supports otherwise. Nothing here shows dubbing is better.
- If listening supports it, the candidate default is **the live prototype** (`balanced`, perhaps with a
  higher base level to survive codecs), because it is the only presentation that already exists in the
  product, adds about 0 to 11 ms of latency, removes most of the dead air, and works under codec
  compression. The replays add buffering and pairing for a benefit nobody has heard yet.
- Headphones only in the first release; speakerphone and phone users stay on AI-only until the echo test is done.
